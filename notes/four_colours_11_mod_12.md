# Four colours are needed for every `d ≡ 11 (mod 12)`

*Working note, 3 October 2026. The proofs below are by hand; every numerical fact they use is rechecked in exact
arithmetic by `data/quadratic_planes/winding/family/structure_lemma.py`, and the configurations are checked by
computer for small `d` (§7). Two internal referees (separate AI agents, with their own programs) checked the proofs
(research log, 3 October); nobody outside the project has. Theorem 1 and Corollary 3 are also proved in Lean 4 with
Mathlib, through a cruder form of Proposition 1 (§7).*

## 1. The results

> **Theorem 1.** Let `d ≡ 11 (mod 12)` be a positive integer. Then `χ(ℚ(√d)²) ≥ 4`.

> **Corollary 2.** For squarefree `d ≥ 2`, the unit-distance graph of `ℚ(√d)²` needs four colours if and only if
> `d ≡ 11 (mod 12)`. Moreover `χ(ℚ(√d)²) = 4` for every squarefree `d ≡ 11 (mod 12)` except possibly those with
> `d ≡ 47, 143, 167 (mod 168)`, for which `χ ≥ 4`.

*Proof of the corollary from Theorem 1.* If `d ≢ 3 (mod 4)`, `χ = 2` (Johnson 1987; Moorhouse, Lemma 8.4 and
Theorem 8.5; Fischer 1990, Theorem 8, which says that `ℚ(√d)²` is 2-colourable exactly when `d ≢ 3 (mod 4)`). If
`d ≡ 3 (mod 4)` and `d ≢ 2 (mod 3)`, `χ ≤ 3` (Fischer 1990, Theorem 9; Moorhouse, Corollary 8.3). If `d ≡ 11 (mod 12)`,
Theorem 1 gives `χ ≥ 4`; and `χ ≤ 4` when `d ≡ 3 (mod 8)` (Fischer 1990, Theorem 10) or `d ≡ 0, 1, 2, 4 (mod 7)`
(Moorhouse, Corollary 8.3). Among `d ≡ 11 (mod 12)`, the residues left by both are `d ≡ 7 (mod 8)` with
`d ≡ 3, 5, 6 (mod 7)`, i.e. `d ≡ 47, 143, 167 (mod 168)`. ∎

> **Corollary 3.** For every prime `p ≥ 5`, `χ(ℚ_p²) ≥ 4`, where `ℚ_p²` is the graph on `ℚ_p²` joining `x` and `y`
> when `(x₁ − y₁)² + (x₂ − y₂)² = 1`. So `χ(ℚ_p²) ≥ 4` exactly for `p ≥ 5`.

*Proof.* As `gcd(p, 12) = 1`, there is a positive integer `d ≡ 11 (mod 12)` with `d ≡ 1 (mod p)` (Chinese remainder
theorem); `d` need not be squarefree, and Theorem 1 is stated for every positive `d ≡ 11 (mod 12)`. By Hensel's lemma
`d` has a square root `s ∈ ℤ_p^×` (the root 1 of `t² − d` modulo `p` is simple), and `√d ↦ s` is a field embedding
`σ : ℚ(√d) → ℚ_p` (`d ≡ 3 (mod 4)` is not a square in `ℚ`). `σ` is an injective ring homomorphism fixing `ℚ`, so
`(σx₁ − σy₁)² + (σx₂ − σy₂)² = σ((x₁ − y₁)² + (x₂ − y₂)²)` equals 1 exactly when the original expression does;
applied coordinatewise, `σ` maps the plane over `ℚ(√d)` (whose edges are the pairs at real distance 1, that is,
with `(x₁ − y₁)² + (x₂ − y₂)² = 1`) isomorphically onto an induced subgraph of `ℚ_p²`. By Theorem 1,
`χ(ℚ_p²) ≥ χ(ℚ(√d)²) ≥ 4`. For `p = 2, 3` no `d ≡ 11 (mod 12)` is a square in `ℚ_p`, and indeed `χ(ℚ₂²) = 2`,
`χ(ℚ₃²) = 3` (both following from Madore 2015). ∎

For `p ≡ 1 (mod 4)` this is weaker than `χ(ℚ_p²) = ∞` (Davies's theorem, `notes/quadratic_planes.md` §5); the new
content is `p ≡ 3 (mod 4)`. The 27 explicit graphs of `notes/quadratic_planes.md` gave `χ(ℚ_p²) ≥ 4` only for
`7 ≤ p < 2 129 503 819`, and by Chebotarev's theorem no finite set of fields can reach every `p`; here `d` depends on
`p`. A finite 4-chromatic unit-distance graph in `ℚ_p²` exists (de Bruijn–Erdős, or König's lemma, as the graph is
countable and locally finite), but it is not exhibited. The second referee checked the corollary (research log,
3 October); for `p = 2 129 503 819`, the first prime the 27 graphs miss, `d = 21 295 038 191` works.

So this answers, for the step from three to four colours, Moorhouse's question (2010) "What can we say about `χ(K²)`
when `K = ℚ(√d)`?": before, the largest known lower bound for a real quadratic field was 3
(`notes/quadratic_planes.md` §1). For `d ≡ 47 (mod 168)`, in particular `d = 47` (Moorhouse's second question), it
gives `χ(ℚ(√47)²) ≥ 4`. `d ≡ 11 (mod 12)` is never a square, and its squarefree part is again `≡ 11 (mod 12)` (and
`≡ 23 (mod 24)` if `d` is). By de Bruijn–Erdős each field contains a finite unit-distance graph with no proper
3-colouring; deleting vertices one at a time (each deletion lowers `χ` by at most 1) gives a finite subgraph with
`χ = 4` exactly. It is triangle-free, because `√3 ∉ ℚ(√d)`.

The proof gives explicit finite sets of unit vectors. Let `G_N` be the set of the `4(2k + 1)` rational unit vectors
whose denominators divide `N = 5^k`, and for an integer `n` let

    u_n = (n + i√d)²/(n² + d) = ((n² − d)/(n² + d), 2n√d/(n² + d)) ∈ ℚ(√d)²,

a unit vector, and `ū_n` its mirror image `((n² − d)/(n² + d), −2n√d/(n² + d))`.

> **Theorem 1a** (`d ≡ 23 (mod 24)`). If `d < 21·5^{k−1}` (for example if `5^k ≥ d/4`), no character of the
> group generated by `U = G_N ∪ G_N u₁ ∪ G_N ū₁` maps every vector of `U` into `[1/3, 2/3]` modulo 1.
>
> **Theorem 1b** (`d ≡ 11 (mod 24)`). Let `s = v₃(d + 1) ≥ 1` and let `n` be a positive integer with `n ≡ 3 (mod 4)` and
> `n ≡ 1 (mod 3^{s+1})`, for example `n = 1 + 2·3^{s+1}`. If `5^k > (2√10/3)·n(n + d)`, no character of the group
> generated by `U = G_N ∪ G_N u₁ ∪ G_N ū₁ ∪ G_N u_n` maps every vector of `U` into `[1/3, 2/3]` modulo 1.

By Theorem W (`notes/winding_lemma.md`), `Cay(ℤU, ±U)` is then not 3-colourable; it is a subgraph of the
unit-distance graph of `ℚ(√d)²`, so Theorems 1a and 1b give Theorem 1. (`−ū₁ = ((d − 1)/(d + 1), 2√d/(d + 1))`;
signs do not matter, because `U` and the condition are symmetric.)

**Earlier work.** The vector `u₁` is classical. It gives the odd closed walk
`((d + 1)/4)(u₁ + ū₁) + ((d − 1)/2)·(1, 0) = 0` of length `d`, which re-proves `χ ≥ 3` for `d ≡ 3 (mod 4)`
(Fischer 1990, Theorem 8; Moorhouse's Theorem 8.6 gives `χ ≥ 3` when `ℚ(√d)` contains `√p` for a prime
`p ≡ 3 (mod 4)`, by a cycle from Pell's equation). Up to sign and mirror image `u₁` is the vector `(b + i√N)/a`,
`a = (N + 1)/2`, `b = (N − 1)/2`, with which Fischer (1990, Theorem 10(ii)) showed that for `N ≡ −1 (mod 24)` the
vectors `(1/2, 0)` and `(1/3, 0)` are sums of unit vectors, so that `ℚ(√N)²` has no *additive* `k`-colouring for
`k ≤ 6` (in Fischer's sense: one induced by a homomorphism from the group `C₀` generated by the unit vectors to a
group of order `k`; his Theorem 1 extends colourings of `C₀` to the plane by cosets). Theorem 1a removes the word
*additive* for three colours. The new ingredients are Theorem W, which turns a 3-colouring into an `ℝ/ℤ`-valued
character, the rotations by `G_N`, whose effect on characters is described exactly by Proposition 1 below, and, for
`d ≡ 11 (mod 24)`, the second vector `u_n`, which supplies the 2-adic information that `u₁` lacks there.

## 2. Characters as complex numbers

Identify `ℚ²` with `ℚ(i)`, and write a vector of `ℚ(√d)²` as `X + √d Y` with `X, Y ∈ ℚ(i)`: the vector
`(X₁ + √d Y₁, X₂ + √d Y₂)` corresponds to `X = X₁ + iX₂`, `Y = Y₁ + iY₂`. For `γ ∈ ℚ(i)` with `|γ| = 1`,
multiplication by `γ` (on `X` and on `Y`) is the rotation by `γ`, an isometry of `ℚ(√d)²`; so `G_N U₀ ⊂ ℚ(√d)²`
consists of unit vectors when `U₀` does. In this notation `u_n = X + √d Y` with `X = (n² − d)/(n² + d)` and
`Y = 2ni/(n² + d)`.

Let `U` be one of the sets of Theorem 1a or 1b, `N = 5^k`, and `T` the least common multiple of the denominators
`n² + d` of the vectors `u_n` used. Every vector of `U` lies in `Γ′ = M + √d M` with `M = (1/(NT))ℤ[i]`: for
`γ ∈ G_N` we have `Nγ ∈ ℤ[i]`, and `γu_n = γX + √d γY` with `X, Y ∈ (1/T)ℤ[i]`. Every character `ξ` of `ℤU` extends to
`Γ′` (the circle group is divisible). The characters of `Γ′ ≅ ℤ⁴` are `X + √d Y ↦ Re(c̄₁X) + Re(c̄₂Y) (mod 1)` with
`c₁, c₂ ∈ ℂ`: `M` has the ℤ-basis `1/(NT)`, `i/(NT)`, on which `Re(c̄/(NT)) = Re(c)/(NT)` and
`Re(c̄ i/(NT)) = Im(c)/(NT)` take arbitrary real values. For a vector `v = X + √d Y` put
`w_v = c₁X̄ + c₂Ȳ ∈ ℂ`. Then for every `γ ∈ G_N`

    ξ(γv) = Re(w̄_v γ)   (mod 1),

and `v ↦ w_v` is ℚ-linear: a linear relation with rational coefficients among vectors `v` holds, exactly in `ℂ`,
among the numbers `w_v`. Let `I = [1/3, 2/3]` and

    S_N = {c ∈ ℂ : Re(c̄ γ) ∈ I + ℤ for every γ ∈ G_N}.

If `ξ(U) ⊆ I + ℤ`, then `w_v ∈ S_N` for every `v ∈ {1, u₁, ū₁, u_n}` (where `1 = (1, 0)`).
`S_N` is invariant under `Nℤ[i]` (because `Nγ ∈ ℤ[i]`), under `c ↦ −c` (because `−I = I` modulo 1) and under
multiplication by `i` (because `iG_N = G_N`).

## 3. The shape of `S_N`

Here `G_N` and `S_N` are defined for every `k ≥ 0` (for `k = 0`, `G_1 = {±1, ±i}`). Notation:
`ρ = (3 + 4i)/5 = (2 + i)/(2 − i)`; `c*_N = N(1 + i)/2`; `Q_N = {(N/3)(α + βi) : α, β ∈ {1, 2}}`;
`Sq₀ = {x + iy : |x|, |y| ≤ 1/6}`; for `k ≥ 0`,

    P_k = {x ∈ ℂ : x̄ ρ^j ∈ Sq₀ for every integer j with |j| ≤ k},

a closed convex polygon (`P₀ = Sq₀`), and `P_k ⊆ P₁` for `k ≥ 1`. Write `Λ₊ = ((2 + i)/5)ℤ[i]` and
`Λ₋ = ((2 − i)/5)ℤ[i]`; both contain `ℤ[i]` with index 5.

**Lemma 1.** `G_N = {ε ρ^j : ε ∈ {1, i, −1, −i}, |j| ≤ k}`; it has `4(2k + 1)` elements.

*Proof.* Let `γ ∈ G_N` and write `γ = α/5^t` with `α ∈ ℤ[i]` and `t ≤ k` least. Then `|α|² = 25^t`, so
`α = ε(2 + i)^e(2 − i)^f` with `e + f = 2t`. If `t ≥ 1`, minimality excludes `e, f ≥ 1` (else `5 | α`), so
`γ = ε(2 ± i)^{2t}/5^t = ερ^{±t}`. The `ερ^j` are distinct, because `ρ^j` has exact denominator `5^{|j|}`. ∎

**Lemma 2.** (a) For every `γ ∈ G_N`, `c̄*_N γ ∈ (1 + i)/2 + ℤ[i]`; so `c*_N ∈ S_N`, and `c*_N + x ∈ S_N` if and only
if `x̄γ ∈ Sq₀ + ℤ[i]` for every `γ ∈ G_N`. In particular `c*_N + P_k ⊆ S_N`.
(b) `Q_N ⊆ S_N`.

*Proof.* (a) By Lemma 1, `c̄*_N γ = (5^{k−|j|}/2)(1 − i)επ` with `π = (2 ± i)^{2|j|}`. `επ` has odd norm, so
`επ ≡ 1 (mod 1 + i)` and `(1 − i)επ ≡ 1 − i (mod 2)`: both coordinates are odd. Since `5^{k−|j|}` is odd,
`c̄*_N γ ∈ (1 + i)/2 + ℤ[i]`. Then `Re` and `Im` of `(c*_N + x)‾γ` lie in `1/2 + Re(x̄γ) + ℤ` and
`1/2 + Im(x̄γ) + ℤ`, and `I = 1/2 + [−1/6, 1/6]`; the conditions for `γ` and `iγ` together say `x̄γ ∈ Sq₀ + ℤ[i]`.
(b) For `q = (N/3)(α + βi)`, `q̄γ = (5^{k−|j|}/3)(α − βi)επ`. In `ℤ[i]/3 = 𝔽₉` the elements with both coordinates
nonzero, `±1 ± i`, are the non-squares (`1 + i` generates `𝔽₉^×` and `(1 + i)μ₄ = {±1 ± i}`). `α − βi` is a
non-square and `επ` is a nonzero square (`ε ∈ μ₄ = (𝔽₉^×)²` and `π` is a square), so `(α − βi)επ` has both
coordinates prime to 3; since `5^{k−|j|}` is prime to 3, both coordinates of `q̄γ` lie in `{1/3, 2/3} + ℤ`. ∎

**Lemma 3 (two numbers).** (a) `max_{x ∈ P₁} |x| = √10/18 ≈ 0.1757`.
(b) Every point of `(Λ₊ ∪ Λ₋) ∖ ℤ[i]` lies at distance at least `√2/6 ≈ 0.2357` from `Sq₀ + ℤ[i]`, and no point of
`(Λ₊ ∪ Λ₋) ∖ ℤ[i]` lies in `[−1/3, 1/3]² + ℤ[i]`.

*Proof.* (a) `x̄ρ^j ∈ Sq₀` says `|⟨x, ρ^j⟩| ≤ 1/6` and `|⟨x, iρ^j⟩| ≤ 1/6`, so `P₁` is cut out by the six slabs
`|⟨x, ν⟩| ≤ 1/6` with `ν ∈ {1, i, ρ, iρ, ρ̄, iρ̄}`. Their directions modulo `180°` are `0°, 36.87°, 53.13°, 90°,
126.87°, 143.13°`; consecutive gaps are `θ = arccos(4/5) ≈ 36.87°` (four times) and `90° − 2θ` (twice). So `P₁` is
a 12-gon circumscribed about the circle of radius `1/6`, with vertices at distance `(1/6)/cos(θ/2) =
(1/6)/(3/√10) = √10/18` (for example `(1/6, 1/18)`) and `(1/6)/cos(45° − θ) < √10/18`.
(b) `Λ₊/ℤ[i]` has the four nonzero classes of `±(2 + i)/5 = ±(2/5 + i/5)` and `±(−1 + 2i)/5`; their representatives
in `(−1/2, 1/2)²` have one coordinate `±2/5` and the other `±1/5`. The nearest point of `Sq₀` to `2/5 + i/5` is
`1/6 + i/6`, at distance `√((7/30)² + (1/30)²) = √2/6`; every other translate `Sq₀ + λ`, `λ ≠ 0`, is at distance
`≥ 1 − 2/5 − 1/6 > √2/6`. The coordinate `±2/5` is at distance `2/5 > 1/3` from `ℤ`. `Λ₋` is the mirror image. ∎

**Lemma 4 (unique lifts).** Let `k ≥ 1`, `N = 5^k`, `N′ = 5^{k−1}`. If `c, c′ ∈ S_N` and `c′ − c ∈ N′ℤ[i]`, then
`c′ − c ∈ Nℤ[i]`.

*Proof.* Write `c′ = c + N′λ`. Then `c̄′ρ^k − c̄ρ^k = N′λ̄ρ^k = λ̄(2 + i)^{2k}/5 = λ̄(2 + i)^{2k−1}/(2 − i) ∈ Λ₊`. Both
`c̄ρ^k` and `c̄′ρ^k` lie in `(I + ℤ) + i(I + ℤ)` (conditions for `γ = ρ^k` and `iρ^k`), so their difference lies in
`[−1/3, 1/3]² + ℤ[i]`; by Lemma 3(b) it lies in `ℤ[i]`, which means `(2 − i) | λ̄(2 + i)^{2k−1}`, i.e.
`(2 + i) | λ`. The same argument with `ρ^{−k}` and `Λ₋` gives `(2 − i) | λ`. So `5 | λ`. ∎

**Proposition 1 (shape of `S_N`).** For every `k ≥ 1`,

    S_{5^k} ⊆ (c*_N + P_k + Nℤ[i]) ∪ (Q_N + Nℤ[i]),

with equality by Lemma 2. In particular every `c ∈ S_N` is `e + x` with either `e ∈ c*_N + Nℤ[i]` and `|x| ≤ √10/18`,
or `e ∈ Q_N + Nℤ[i]` and `x = 0`.

*Proof.* By induction on `k`, with `N′ = 5^{k−1}`; note `S_N ⊆ S_{N′}` (since `G_{N′} ⊆ G_N`) and
`c*_N − c*_{N′} = 2N′(1 + i) ∈ N′ℤ[i]`.

*`k = 1`.* `S_1 = (1 + i)/2 + Sq₀ + ℤ[i]`, so `c ∈ S_5` is `c = c*_5 + x + λ` with `x ∈ Sq₀`, `λ ∈ ℤ[i]`. By
Lemma 2(a) (with `N = 5`, `γ = ρ`), `c̄ρ ∈ (I + ℤ) + i(I + ℤ)` says `x̄ρ + λ̄ρ ∈ Sq₀ + ℤ[i]`, where
`λ̄ρ = λ̄(2 + i)/(2 − i) ∈ Λ₊`. If `λ̄ρ ∉ ℤ[i]`, Lemma 3(b) gives `|x| = |x̄ρ| ≥ √2/6`; as `x ∈ Sq₀`, `x` is one of
the corners `(±1 ± i)/6`. The same holds with `ρ̄` and `Λ₋`. So if `x` is not a corner, `λ̄ρ, λ̄ρ̄ ∈ ℤ[i]`, which
gives `5 | λ` as in Lemma 4, and then `x̄ρ^{±1} ∈ Sq₀ + ℤ[i]` with `|x̄ρ^{±1}| ≤ √2/6 < 5/6` gives `x̄ρ^{±1} ∈ Sq₀`:
`c ∈ c*_5 + P₁ + 5ℤ[i]`. If `x` is a corner, `c ≡ (1 + i)/2 + (±1 ± i)/6 = (α + βi)/3 (mod ℤ[i])` with
`α, β ∈ {1, 2}`. The point `q = (5/3)(α′ + β′i) ∈ Q_5` with `α′ ≡ 2α`, `β′ ≡ 2β (mod 3)` is congruent to `c` modulo
`ℤ[i]` and lies in `S_5` (Lemma 2(b)); by Lemma 4, `c ∈ q + 5ℤ[i]`.

*`k ≥ 2`.* Let `c ∈ S_N ⊆ S_{N′}`. If `c ∈ Q_{N′} + N′ℤ[i]`, say `c ≡ (N′/3)(α + βi)`, the point
`q = (N/3)(α′ + β′i) ∈ Q_N` with `α′ ≡ 2α`, `β′ ≡ 2β (mod 3)` satisfies `q ≡ c (mod N′ℤ[i])` and `q ∈ S_N`, so
`c ∈ q + Nℤ[i]` by Lemma 4. Otherwise `c = c*_N + x + N′λ` with `x ∈ P_{k−1} ⊆ P₁` and `λ ∈ ℤ[i]`. As in the case
`k = 1`, `c̄ρ^k ∈ (I + ℤ) + i(I + ℤ)` says `x̄ρ^k + μ ∈ Sq₀ + ℤ[i]` with `μ = N′λ̄ρ^k ∈ Λ₊`. If `μ ∉ ℤ[i]`, Lemma 3
gives `√2/6 ≤ |x| ≤ √10/18`, impossible. So `(2 + i) | λ`, likewise `(2 − i) | λ`, so `c ≡ c*_N + x (mod N)`, and
`x̄ρ^{±k} ∈ Sq₀ + ℤ[i]` with `|x| < 5/6` gives `x̄ρ^{±k} ∈ Sq₀`, so `x ∈ P_k`. ∎

For `k = 1, 2, 3`, `structure_lemma.py` computes `S_{5^k}` directly by exact polygon clipping, independently of the
induction: a 12-gon, 20-gon and 28-gon around `c*_N` (areas `0.08995`, `0.08802`, `0.08780`) and the four points of
`Q_N`. As `k → ∞`, `P_k` decreases to the disc of radius `1/6`.

**Types.** By Proposition 1, write each `c ∈ S_N` as `c = Nε + x` with `|x| ≤ r := √10/18` and `ε` of one of two
*types*: type `c` if `ε ∈ (1 + i)/2 + ℤ[i]`, type `q` if `ε ∈ (α + βi)/3 + ℤ[i]` with `α, β ∈ {1, 2}`. In both cases
`Nε ∈ (N/6)ℤ[i]`. For a prime `p`, let `ℤ_(p)[i] ⊂ ℚ(i)` be the elements whose denominators are prime to `p`, and
`v₃` the 3-adic valuation of `ℚ(i)` (3 is inert in `ℤ[i]`). Then:
- type `q` ⟺ `v₃(ε) = −1` (the numerator `α + βi + 3λ` is not divisible by 3); type `c` ⟹ `v₃(ε) ≥ 0`;
- type `c` ⟺ `ε ∉ ℤ_(2)[i]`, and then `ε ≡ h := (1 + i)/2 (mod ℤ_(2)[i])`; type `q` ⟹ `ε ∈ ℤ_(2)[i]`;
- for an odd integer `m`, `m h ≡ h (mod ℤ_(2)[i])`, and `2h ∈ ℤ[i]`.

**Lemma 5 (exact relations).** Let `σ₁, …, σ_ℓ ∈ S_N` and let `m₁, …, m_ℓ` be integers with `Σ m_j σ_j = 0`.
Write `σ_j = Nε_j + x_j` as above. If `N > 6r Σ |m_j|`, then `Σ m_j ε_j = 0`.

*Proof.* `Σ m_j Nε_j = −Σ m_j x_j`. The left side lies in `(N/6)ℤ[i]`, whose nonzero elements have modulus
`≥ N/6`; the right side has modulus `≤ r Σ|m_j| < N/6`. So both are 0. ∎

## 4. Proof of Theorem 1a (`d ≡ 23 (mod 24)`)

Put `a = (d + 1)/4` and `b = (d − 1)/2`: `a ≡ 0 (mod 6)`, `b` is odd and `3 ∤ b`. The vectors satisfy
`a(u₁ + ū₁) + b·1 = 0`, so `z = w_1` (for the vector `1 = (1, 0)`), `w = w_{u₁}`, `w′ = w_{ū₁}` satisfy

    a(w + w′) = −b z,   z, w, w′ ∈ S_N.                                             (1)

Write `z = e₃ + y`, `w = e₁ + x₁`, `w′ = e₂ + x₂` with `e_j ∈ (c*_N + Nℤ[i]) ∪ (Q_N + Nℤ[i])`, `x₁, x₂, y ∈ P_k`,
and `x_j = 0` or `y = 0` when the corresponding `e` lies in `Q_N + Nℤ[i]` (Proposition 1; `0 ∈ P_k`). Then

    a(e₁ + e₂) + b e₃ = −(a x₁ + a x₂ + b y).                                       (2)

*Left side.* `a c*_N = (a/2)N(1 + i) ∈ Nℤ[i]` because `a` is even, and `a(N/3)(α + βi) ∈ Nℤ[i]` because `3 | a`;
so `a(e₁ + e₂) ∈ Nℤ[i]`. If `e₃ ∈ c*_N + Nℤ[i]`, then `b e₃ ∈ c*_N + Nℤ[i]` because `b` is odd; if
`e₃ ∈ Q_N + Nℤ[i]`, then `b e₃ ∈ Q_N + Nℤ[i]` because `3 ∤ b`. So the left side `u + iv` lies in `c*_N + Nℤ[i]`,
where `|u|, |v| ≥ N/2`, or in `Q_N + Nℤ[i]` (`−Q_N ≡ Q_N`), where `|u|, |v| ≥ N/3`. In both cases
`3|u| + 4|v| ≥ 7N/2` resp. `≥ 7N/3`.

*Right side.* `P_k` is convex and symmetric, so `a x₁ + a x₂ + b y ∈ (2a + b)P_k = d P_k` in the first case, and
`a x₁ + a x₂ ∈ 2a P_k = ((d + 1)/2) P_k` in the second (`y = 0`). On `P₁ ⊇ P_k` the slabs for `ρ` and `ρ̄` give
`|3u ± 4v| ≤ 5/6`, so `3|u| + 4|v| = max |3u ± 4v| ≤ 5/6`.

*Comparison.* The first case needs `7N/2 ≤ 5d/6`, i.e. `d ≥ 21N/5`; the second needs `7N/3 ≤ 5(d + 1)/12`, i.e.
`d + 1 ≥ 28N/5`. If `d < 21N/5 = 21·5^{k−1}`, neither holds (`d + 1 < 21N/5 + 1 < 28N/5`). If `5^k ≥ d/4`, then
`d ≤ 4N < 21N/5`. ∎

**The bound is sharp for `k ≤ 5`, and not for `k ≥ 6`** (corrected 4 October, after a third reading). If
`d ≥ 21N/5` and `k ≤ 5`, then `c*_N/d ∈ P_k`, as the conditions at `ρ^{±1}` are the binding ones, and this gives a
character with all three types c. At `k = 6`, `(1 − i)ρ⁶ = (1457 − 22049i)/15625`, so `c*_N/d ∈ P_6` only when
`d ≥ 66147N/15625`, and for `N = 5⁶` an exact decision (all type choices and all lattice points, given
Proposition 1) finds no character for the 22 values `65 639 ≤ d ≤ 66 143` with `d ≡ 23 (mod 24)`, although
`d ≥ 21N/5 = 65 625`; the first value with a character is `66 167` (`family/fc_case23.py`). An exact computation by the
first referee (cells of `S_N`, Minkowski sums and the lattice
points of `Nℤ[i]`, cross-checked by a second method, every feasible case with an explicit character checked on all of
`U`) decided the question for every `d ≡ 23 (mod 24)` up to 1295 (`N = 5, 25, 125`) and up to 2999 (`N = 625`): the
set of Theorem 1a has no character into `[1/3, 2/3]` exactly when `d < 21N/5`. For `N = 25` the infeasible values are
`d = 23, 47, 71, 95`, for `N = 125` the values `d ≤ 503`, for `N = 625` the values `d ≤ 2615`; for `N = 5` every
`d ≥ 23` is feasible.

## 5. Proof of Theorem 1b (`d ≡ 11 (mod 24)`)

Now `a = (d + 1)/4` is odd, and the argument of §4 fails: `a + b` is even and `3 | a`, so
`a(c*_N + q) + b c*_N ∈ Nℤ[i]` for `q ∈ Q_N`, and since `gcd(a, b) = 1` suitable lifts `z = c*_N + Nλ₃`,
`w = c*_N + Nλ₁`, `w′ = q + Nλ₂` solve (1) exactly. So `G_N ∪ G_N u₁ ∪ G_N ū₁` has a character into `[1/3, 2/3]`
for every `k` (the first referee confirmed this exactly for `N = 5, 25, 125` and `d ≤ 299`). The vector `u_n`
removes these characters. Put `y = w_{u_n}`. The vectors satisfy, besides `a(u₁ + ū₁) + b·1 = 0`,

    (n² + d) u_n = (n − 1)(n + d)·1 + n(1 + d) u₁

(compare `X` and `Y`: `(n − 1)(n + d) + n(1 − d) = n² − d` and `n(1 + d)·2/(1 + d) = 2n`). So

    a(w + w′) + b z = 0,      (n² + d) y − (n − 1)(n + d) z − n(1 + d) w = 0.           (3)

As `n ≥ 1`, all coefficients are nonnegative, and the sums of their absolute values are `2a + b = d` and
`2n(n + d)`. (For negative `n` the second sum is larger and the hypothesis must be changed; the second referee
found an exact counterexample with `n = −17`, `d = 59`, `N = 5`.) Since
`N > (2√10/3) n(n + d) = 12r·n(n + d) ≥ 6r·d`, Lemma 5 applies to both relations: writing `z = Nε_z + x_z`,
`w = Nε_w + x_w`, `w′ = Nε_{w′} + x_{w′}`, `y = Nε_y + x_y`,

    a(ε_w + ε_{w′}) = −b ε_z,      (n² + d) ε_y = (n − 1)(n + d) ε_z + n(1 + d) ε_w,     (4)

exactly in `ℚ(i)`.

*Step 1: `ε_z` has type `c`, and `v₃(ε_z) ≥ s − 1`.* `v₃(a) = s` and `3 ∤ b` (`b ≡ 2 (mod 3)`), and
`v₃(ε_w + ε_{w′}) ≥ −1` because both lie in `(1/6)ℤ[i]`. So `v₃(ε_z) ≥ s − 1 ≥ 0`, and `ε_z` is not of type `q`.

*Step 2 (at 2): `ε_y` has type `c` if and only if `ε_w` has type `q`.* As `d ≡ 3 (mod 8)` and `n ≡ 3 (mod 4)`:
`n² + d ≡ 4 (mod 8)`, `n(1 + d)` has `1 + d ≡ 4 (mod 8)`, and `n − 1 ≡ 2 (mod 4)`, `n + d ≡ 2 (mod 4)`. So the three
coefficients of the second relation (4) are 4 times odd integers `A′, B′, C′`, and `A′ε_y = B′ε_z + C′ε_w`. Modulo
`ℤ_(2)[i]`, `B′ε_z ≡ h` (Step 1) and `C′ε_w ≡ h` or `0` according as `ε_w` has type `c` or `q`. So
`A′ε_y ≡ 2h ≡ 0` if `ε_w` has type `c`, and `≡ h` if it has type `q`; as `A′` is odd, `ε_y ∈ ℤ_(2)[i]` exactly when
`ε_w` has type `c`.

*Step 3 (at 3): `ε_y` has type `q` if and only if `ε_w` has type `q`.* Let `e = v₃(n − 1) ≥ s + 1`; `3 ∤ n`,
`3 ∤ n + 1`. Then `n² + d = (n − 1)(n + 1) + (d + 1)` and `n + d = (n − 1) + (d + 1)` have `v₃ = s`, `n(1 + d)` has
`v₃ = s`, and `(n − 1)(n + d)` has `v₃ = e + s`. Divide the second relation (4) by `3^s`:
`A″ε_y = B″ε_z + C″ε_w` with `3 ∤ A″C″` and `v₃(B″ε_z) ≥ e + s − 1 ≥ 2s ≥ 2`. So `v₃(ε_y) = −1` if and only if
`v₃(ε_w) = −1`.

Steps 2 and 3 contradict each other whatever the type of `ε_w`. So no character exists. ∎

Only Step 1 uses `ū₁`. One vector cannot do the work of `u₁` and `u_n` together: a vector `(μ + iν√d)/τ` with
`μ² + dν² = τ²` in lowest terms has `τ ≢ 0 (mod 4)` when `d ≡ 3 (mod 8)` (if `τ` is even, `μ` and `ν` are odd and
`μ² + dν² ≡ 4 (mod 8)`), while the argument of §4 needs the coefficient `τ/gcd(τ, 2μ)` of the relation
`τ(v + v̄) = 2μ·1` to be even, that is `4 | τ`. In `ℚ₂(√d)` the form `x² + y²` is anisotropic for `d ≡ 3 (mod 8)` and
isotropic for `d ≡ 7 (mod 8)`. Pairs of vectors do not suffice either: for `d = 11` no pair of vectors
`(μ + iν√11)/τ` (`μ² + 11ν² = τ²`, `ν ≤ 7`, `τ ≤ 60`) together with `G_N` gives an infeasible configuration for large
`N`, while 28 triples do (14 up to the sign of `μ`; §7). The conditions on `n` are sufficient, not necessary: for
`d = 11` the contradiction of Steps 2 and 3 also holds for `n = 7, 17, 29, 43, …`.

## 6. What the proofs use

Theorem W, in the direction "3-colourable ⟹ a character with `ξ(S) ⊆ [1/3, 2/3]`", for the group `ℤU ≅ ℤ⁴`
(`notes/winding_lemma.md`; its proof for infinite groups averages with an invariant mean and is not constructive;
the Lean proof, `lean/TheoremWInf.lean`, averages over boxes and takes a limit along an ultrafilter);
the elementary arithmetic of `ℤ[i]` (Lemmas 1–4); and the explicit constants `√10/18` and `√2/6` (Lemma 3). The
condition on `N` is generous: for `d = 11` (`n = 19`) the proof asks for `N = 3125`, but the configuration with
`N = 25` (40 vectors up to sign) is already infeasible.

## 7. Checks

  `d ≡ 11 (mod 12)` (`FourColours.not_colorable_three`: the unit-distance graph of `ℚ(√d)²` is not 3-colourable), and
  `lean/PadicFour.lean` proves Corollary 3 (`PadicFour.padic_not_colorable_three_unconditional`) and its form for every
  field of characteristic 0 containing a square root of some `d ≡ 11 (mod 12)`. Theorem W for an arbitrary abelian
  group and finite `S` is `lean/TheoremWInf.lean` (box averages of the winding sums, a limit along an ultrafilter; also
  for `K_{p/q}`, `p < 4q`). The project's Lean workflow builds the files, compares their axioms with
  `lean/axioms.expected` (only `propext`, `Classical.choice`, `Quot.sound`) and replays them in Lean's kernel with
  `leanchecker`; on the commit that brought them in (ec7b021a) it passed on a separate machine, because the
  repository's CI minutes were exhausted (`lean/VERIFY_ec7b021a.md`). The formal proof uses a cruder form of Proposition 1, which suffices for Theorem 1 but not for the
  sharp bound of Theorem 1a: every `w ∈ S_N` is `N((1 + i)/2 + t) + x` with `t ∈ ℤ[i]` and `|x|² < 1/18`, or
  `N((α + βi)/3 + t)` with `α, β ∈ {1, 2}`. The induction starts at `k = 0`, where `S_1 = ([1/3, 2/3] + ℤ)²` and the
